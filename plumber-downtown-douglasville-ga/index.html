Downtown Douglasville is the older center of the city, built around the railroad that runs east to west through it. This page is for people who live or work in and around the historic core and want a plumber for a leak, a slow drain or a water heater that has quit. The sections below separate what is documented about downtown from general plumbing information and from what we do not know.

What is documented about downtown

The town was established by the Georgia General Assembly on February 25, 1875, and regular train service to Atlanta began on May 15, 1882. The downtown core is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, according to the city's own history page.

Buildings.Most are one- and two-story brick buildings from the mid-19th to the mid-20th century.
Public spaces.O'Neal Plaza and Hunter Park are downtown gathering places.
Railroad.The line still runs east to west through the middle of downtown.
Neighborhood name.Real estate sites list Downtown Douglasville as its own neighborhood.

Age tells you little until someone looks

A building from the early 1900s might have its original pipes, a mix of repairs or a full repipe from a decade ago. Age alone does not say which. The reliable way to find out is to look, with an access point, a camera down the drain or a trace of the supply line.

That is why a visit here starts with questions about what has already been replaced. Tell dispatch what you know about past work, even if it is only that the water heater is new. A sewer camera inspection or leak detection visit turns a guess into a finding.

General information for shared and historic buildings

General plumbing information, not a downtown finding: buildings with several units often share a main shutoff. Find out which valve serves your space before something goes wrong, and tell the owner or manager first, since a shared line may be theirs to repair.

Work that changes the outside of a building in a historic district may need city review. The city has a Historic Preservation Commission, so ask Community Development before planning exterior changes.

If water is running and you cannot stop it

Four moves protect the building while help is on the way.

  1. 1

    Find the nearest shutoff. In a shared building, that may be a valve in a utility closet or at the street.

  2. 2

    Cut power near water. Use the breaker for the wet area and stay out of standing water.

  3. 3

    Move what you can. Lift files, electronics and stock off the floor.

  4. 4

    Call. Give the street address, the floor and what you can see.

What we have not verified

We have no record of which downtown blocks share service lines, what pipe materials sit inside a given building, or how old a given building's plumbing is. We do not publish guesses about those. Your own building will answer them once a plumber is on site.
